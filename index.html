Quick Take

A grieving Chicago-area construction worker is pulled into a scrappy community-theater production of Romeo and Juliet, and the play’s tragedy begins to echo the loss his family has been unable to name. Co-directed by Kelly O’Sullivan and Alex Thompson, it is a small, funny, and devastating drama about how rehearsal-room vulnerability can reopen a household locked in silence.

Director — Alex Thompson

Chicago filmmaker and O’Sullivan’s partner; directed Saint Frances and co-produced Ghostlight, sharing the monitor and day-to-day direction with O’Sullivan. Other notable works: Święta Frances, Obchód (Rounding).

Sole screenwriter; wrote Dan for Keith Kupferer after working with him on stage in The Humans (Chicago, 2014), and built the story around a middle-aged man’s confrontation with a Romeo and Juliet-shaped family tragedy. Other notable works: Święta Frances.

Dan’s wife, a school teacher and theater director, still navigating the family’s lawsuit and her own blocked mourning; played by Kupferer’s real-life partner, a Chicago stage veteran with few Filmweb-indexed screen leads beyond this film.

Story Overview

Construction worker Dan Mueller is under crushing stress: his teenage daughter Daisy is in constant disciplinary trouble at school, and the family is pursuing a wrongful-death lawsuit against Christine Hawthorne, the ex-girlfriend of Dan and Sharon’s late son Brian, who died by suicide. At work, Dan assaults a rude motorist; Rita, an actor from a community theater across the street, sees the outburst and invites him inside to read for Lord Capulet in their upcoming Romeo and Juliet.

Dan starts soliciting acting advice from Daisy, who once loved theater and has since withdrawn from it. Tension at home spikes when Sharon, a teacher and school theater director, tries to build a garden over the yard site associated with Brian’s suicide and Dan erupts at Daisy for walking across it. Back at the theater, Dan watches Rita (Juliet) clash with the actor playing Romeo, who complains she is too old for the part. After seeing the play’s double suicide rehearsed, Dan tells the cast about Brian’s death; Daisy and Sharon later see Rita embracing him outside the theater and misread the intimacy.

Dan commits more time to the production and is eventually cast as Romeo. Put on mandatory leave at his construction job, he lies to his family about where he goes. Daisy follows him, discovers the theater secret, and quickly bonds with the cast. At home, Sharon—having learned from Dan’s coworker that he is on leave—accuses him of infidelity, forcing him to admit the truth. Moved by his commitment and talent, Sharon lets the company perform in the school gym for a one-night show and Daisy is cast as Mercutio.

As opening night nears, Dan cannot get through Romeo’s suicide; Daisy urges him to imagine Brian’s feelings. At the deposition with the Hawthornes, Dan’s testimony reveals that on the night of Daisy’s last play, Brian and Christine attempted suicide together because her family was moving and he was not allowed to go with her. Dan realizes he has blamed himself rather than Christine, and he says she is not at fault—effectively tanking the lawsuit. Sharon is furious that he wasted their money and blocked her and Daisy from grieving on the terms they thought they needed.

At the performance, Dan and Rita’s finale moves Sharon to tears. As Romeo dies, Dan glimpses Brian in the shadows offstage. After the show the Muellers embrace, attend the cast party together, and go home—still grieving, but no longer sealed off from one another.

Context

What circumstances shaped the making of this movie?

  • O’Sullivan and Thompson pivoted to Ghostlight when the 2023 WGA/SAG-AFTRA strikes halted their higher-budget next project Mouse; with an interim agreement they shot locally in Chicago-area suburbs (notably Waukegan, Highland Park, Deerfield) in October 2023 on a reported budget just under $500,000, editing toward a cut submitted to Sundance within days of wrap.
  • Casting began with Keith Kupferer (O’Sullivan’s former stage castmate from The Humans); he asked whether his daughter Katherine could audition for Daisy, and casting director Mickie Pascal and the filmmakers then brought in Tara Mallen so the on-screen Mueller family was played by a real family—only Dolly de Leon was flown in from outside the Chicago theater community.
  • Production design and costumes leaned on borrowed theater stock (Raven Theatre, Three Brothers Theatre in Waukegan, and others); actors largely wore their own clothes, Shakespeare costumes were donated tubs of stock pieces, and Three Brothers doubled as holding/rehearsal space beside the storefront theater location—O’Sullivan was roughly eight months pregnant (and at one point isolated with COVID, directing via monitor/walkie) while they shot and cut at breakneck speed.

What deeper themes or ideas does the movie explore?

  • Grief as a family system that isolates each member differently—Dan’s bottled rage, Sharon’s practical mourning, Daisy’s explosive acting-out—until a shared artistic ritual forces them into the same emotional room (Filmmaker Magazine interview; Variety; LA Times).
  • Community theater as therapy and sanctuary: rehearsal games, ensemble hugs, and “fake” feelings that somehow become real enough to unlock what talk therapy and a lawsuit could not (Variety; IndieWire; Filmmaker).
  • Constricted masculinity—anger as the only sanctioned male emotion—and the slow work of feeling without suddenly becoming fluent in therapeutic language (O’Sullivan in Filmmaker Magazine).
  • Art imitating life: Romeo and Juliet’s young lovers’ suicide is not a cute parallel but the narrative’s withheld hinge, revealed so Dan must empathize with the choice he could not stop (Wikipedia plot; Variety; Guardian/IndieWire framing of delayed revelation).
  • Authenticity of scrappy collaborative making—real Chicago ensemble chemistry, donated costumes, real family dynamics—as both production method and thematic statement about how healing happens in imperfect rooms (IndieWire; Chicago Tribune).
